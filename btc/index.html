Projection Ladder

The same model rolled up over eight horizons. Every net figure carries its P10 to P90 band underneath, because a mining P&L four years out is a distribution, not a number.

CoinWarz prints a break-even of 6,832 days and days-to-mine-1-BTC of 4,288 to four significant figures. Neither number can survive one difficulty retarget. Precision without an interval is a claim you cannot defend in an interview.

Grain warning, the thing that breaks these tables

Difficulty does not change at midnight. It changes at a block height, every 2,016 blocks, and that block lands whenever it lands. Roughly 24 days a year, a single calendar day runs under two different difficulties. A daily-grain fact table silently prices those whole days at one of them. That is the bug that cost me two weeks of payback accuracy, see the Data Model section.

04
Depreciation and Resale

What the machines are worth after a year of mining, what the books say they are worth, and why those two numbers are not the same thing.

Book depreciation is a policy choice that hits earnings and tax. Market value is what somebody will actually pay you. An ASIC does not decay on a straight line; it is worth whatever its remaining earning power is worth to a buyer, which is why old rigs go to zero the week hashprice drops below their power cost rather than gliding gently to salvage.

05
Valuation Engine

Four families of mining-based valuation, weighted, normalised, and pooled into one posterior, plus a fifth that gates confidence rather than moving the number. Drag the weights; if the verdict flips at plausible weights, the verdict is soft and you should say so out loud.

Five of the eight published mining valuation models are the same trade wearing different hats, they all resolve to (hashrate × efficiency × power price) ÷ issuance. Averaging eight raw signals silently hands 70% of the weight to one factor. So they are grouped into families first, and only families are weighted.

Family weights
Weights sum to 1.00 and renormalise as you drag. Regime is a gate, not a summand, it scales confidence, it does not move fair value.

Where the Price Came From

Every machine price on this page, traced to a source, a date and a kind. Click any machine in the console to change what is shown here.

Almost all public ASIC price data is asking price, not sale price. A listing is an offer nobody has agreed to pay. The only free, dated, lot-sized record of what machines actually traded for is Luxor's RFQ Insights series, which ran April to November 2023 and stopped; their ongoing index is itself ask-derived and sits behind an API key and a paid tier. So nothing here is called a sale unless a source published a fill.

The Data Model

Everything above is a query against a Kimball star. This section is the schema itself, grain statements, keys, additivity, and the decisions I would have to defend.

A calculator that hard-codes its arithmetic answers one question. A dimensional model answers the questions nobody has asked yet. That is the entire argument for building it this way, and it is the same argument for a sales-and-revenue warehouse at a food company.

Bus matrix
Business processDateEpochModelSiteVendorContract

Conformed dimensions are the columns. A dimension used by more than one process is the thing that makes cross-process questions answerable, that is what "single source of truth" means in practice.

Architecture decision records
ADR-001 · Difficulty is a dimension, the retarget is a fact

Context. Difficulty is a number that changes. The reflex is to put it on the fact table as a measure.

Decision. Difficulty lives on dim_network_epoch, keyed on the retarget block height. Each epoch is exactly 2,016 blocks. The retarget event itself, the percentage change, the elapsed time, the implied hashrate, is a transaction-grain fact.

Alternatives. (a) Difficulty as a measure on the daily fact: fails, because it is not additive and someone will sum it. (b) A type-2 SCD on a "network" dimension keyed by date: fails, because the epoch boundary is a block height, not midnight.

Consequences. Facts carry an epoch_key as well as a date_key. On a retarget day that means two rows, each with an hours_in_segment measure summing to 24. Every weighted average in the warehouse weights on that column.

ADR-002 · ASIC market price is a fact, not an attribute

Context. An Antminer S19j Pro is 104 TH/s and 3,068 W. Those are attributes. It is also $297 at one vendor and $1,400 at another, today.

Decision. Specs go on dim_miner_model (type 1, a manufacturer respec is a correction, not history worth keeping). Price goes in fct_asic_quote, grain: one row per model per vendor per observation date.

Alternatives. Type-2 the price onto the model dimension. It works for a while and then a machine with daily quotes from nine vendors generates thousands of versions of one row, and every join to it fans out.

Consequences. "What does this cost" becomes a windowed query, not a lookup, and you get median, spread and vendor count for free. That spread is why the table above grades price quality instead of pretending one number is the price.

ADR-003 · Ratios are never stored

Context. I stored avg_hashrate_th_s on a daily fact. Someone summed it in a dashboard and got a fleet hashrate thirty times the site's actual capacity. It sat there for a week, because a big number on a tile looks correct.

Decision. No ratio goes in a fact table. Store the numerator and the denominator, both additive. Hashrate, J/TH, uptime, margin %, cost per BTC are all views that divide SUM(numerator) by SUM(denominator).

Alternatives. Store the ratio and document that it must not be summed. Documentation is not a control.

Consequences. The fact table is wider and duller. Every ratio is correct at every level of rollup automatically, which is the whole point.

ADR-004 · The halving schedule is observed, never derived

Context. Subsidy is 50 / 2^floor(height/210000). It is tempting to compute it.

Decision. dim_halving_era holds it as data, and daily issuance is loaded from observed coinbase outputs rather than computed. A test asserts the two agree.

Consequences. The test caught a real class of bug: blocks where the miner claimed less than the full subsidy. Derived values would have shown production that never existed.

What this model cannot tell you
  • It cannot tell you where Bitcoin's price is going. Every valuation family here is circular: price drives difficulty drives cost, so cost follows price with a two-week to two-month lag rather than anchoring it. These are anchors, not timing signals.
  • Nobody publishes the network's true fleet-average J/TH. The 20 J/TH central estimate is inferred, and it is the single largest source of error in the two families that take it as a direct input, the cost basis and the hashprice payback, which carry 60% of the default weight between them. That is why the band is wide.
  • Three of the four weighted families read hashrate or difficulty as their input, and the fifth read, the regime gate, is built on the same signal. In 2026 public miners have signed more than $70bn of AI and HPC contracts and are moving megawatts off Bitcoin voluntarily. Falling hashrate no longer reliably means miner distress, which breaks the causal chain those models assume. Correlated model failure is the worst case for any composite, and it looks exactly like high confluence.
  • It ignores curtailment and grid-services revenue, demand charges, orphan rate, and pool luck variance beyond the modelled band. Resale value is modelled, in section 04, but from a catalogue cross-section rather than a time series, which is a real weakness stated there.
  • All warehouse data is synthetic. The network snapshot is real and dated; the fleet is a scenario.

On the cost-to-produce spread specifically

Four credible estimates of the same quantity, published within three months of each other, range from $62,650 to $80,000. That is a 28% spread on a number people quote to the dollar. The reason is the one in the panel above: each uses a different assumption for the network's blended machine efficiency, and none of them can measure it. The spread is the honest answer, not the midpoint.
