Is Bitcoin mining worth it?

Mostly no, at prices on 16 August 2026. A fifty unit Antminer S21 Pro build, hosted at 7.5 cents per kWh and held for 36 months, produces one bitcoin for $104,654 against a market of $63,050, and does not repay its $96,900 of hardware inside five years. Of the four scenarios below, only the operator paying 3.6 cents per kWh clears its own cost to produce. Change the configuration and the verdict recomputes.

Configure a build and read the verdict

Five inputs decide whether a Bitcoin mining build pays for itself: which machine, how many, what the power costs, where the machines sit, and how long you hold them. The default below is the one this site models everywhere else, a fifty unit Antminer S21 Pro fleet, hosted at the middle of the published hosting band. The verdict panel is computed by the same engine that baked the numbers into this page, so it agrees with the static text before any script has run.

The spread between the top and bottom row is entirely electricity. Same machine, same purchase price, same difficulty, same hold. Moving from 3.6 cents to 18.44 cents raises the cost of producing one bitcoin from $60,261 to $229,161, a factor of 3.8. No hardware decision available in 2026 moves the number that far. This is the reason miners chase stranded gas, curtailment credits and Paraguayan hydro rather than chasing efficiency alone.

The hosted row is the interesting one, because it is the one most readers can actually buy. At 7.5 cents, the middle of the band the twelve surveyed hosting providers publish, the fleet nets +$21 a month. That is not a margin, it is a rounding error: the shutdown price for this configuration is $62,924, against a market of $63,050. A four percent fall in bitcoin, or half a cent on the rate, puts the machines below the line at which running them costs more than switching them off.

The industrial row is shown because it is the honest ceiling for a self run site, not because a house gets that rate. The US industrial average of 8.71 cents in May 2026 is what a factory pays. A residential meter pays 18.44. Anyone modelling home mining at an industrial tariff is modelling a warehouse they have not rented yet.

What has to be true

The default build needs power below 3.84 cents per kWh, or bitcoin above $105,074, to cover the cost of producing a coin over a 36 month hold from 16 August 2026. Those two conditions are the whole answer: everything else on this page is a way of showing how far the configuration sits from one of them. 04

Hosted or at home

Changing nothing but the building moves the cost of producing a bitcoin by $474 on a base of $104,654, which is 0.5 percent of it. The venue question is therefore not really a venue question. It is a question about which power price each venue can reach, and about a set of costs and risks this model does not price at all.

Hosting buys a rate, not a building. A fifty machine buyer on a residential meter pays 18.44 cents. The same buyer signing a colocation contract reaches the 5.5 to 8.5 cent band that the twelve surveyed operators publish. That gap is worth $124,508 per bitcoin produced, which dwarfs every modelled difference between the two venues. The model gives home a slightly better PUE and a worse uptime, and those two effects very nearly cancel.

What hosting costs you is control. Your machines sit in someone else's building, under someone else's insurance, subject to someone else's curtailment decisions. The hosting survey on this site records one operator that lost access to roughly $30 million of customer equipment when sanctions hit its host, and one that is being sued over machines a customer says were never delivered. Neither risk appears anywhere in the arithmetic above, and neither can be modelled from published data.

What home mining costs you is not in this model either. Fifty S21 Pro units draw 184 kW and reject essentially all of it as heat into whatever room they occupy. That is a three phase service, an electrician, a ventilation plan and a noise problem measured in the seventies of decibels. The realistic home build is one or two machines, and because cost to produce is scale invariant, the verdict for one machine is identical to the verdict for fifty.

05

The halving is inside a three year hold

The block subsidy halves on day 607, which is month 20 of a 36 month hold, so the third year of this build earns 55 percent less bitcoin than the first year for exactly the same electricity. Any payback figure quoted as capital divided by this month's profit is quoting the best month the fleet will ever have.

Whole hold4.460 BTC 100% −$102,362 steps down on day 607

Two forces compound over a 36 month hold and both point the same way. Difficulty drifts upward at 0.20 percent per epoch, which shrinks the fleet's share of the network by roughly 5 percent a year. Then the subsidy halves at block 1,050,000 and the revenue side is cut in half overnight while the electricity bill does not move by a cent. The fleet produces 1.986 BTC in its first twelve months and 0.895 BTC in its last twelve.

This is why a shorter hold is not simply a smaller version of a longer one. Shortening the hold to 12 months at the default power price leaves the cost of producing a coin at $90,522, because the hardware has barely been consumed and the subsidy is still whole. Extending it to 60 months raises it to $119,568. A hold period is not a neutral reporting window; it is an assumption about how much post-halving revenue you are willing to average into your own answer.

06

Questions people actually ask

Is Bitcoin mining profitable in 2026?

Only at a power price most people cannot get. Modelled on 16 August 2026 with bitcoin at $63,050, a fifty unit Antminer S21 Pro fleet held for 36 months produces one bitcoin for $60,261 at 3.6 cents per kWh, $104,654 at 7.5 cents hosted, and $229,161 at the US residential average of 18.44 cents. Only the first of those clears the market price. The break-even power price for this build is 3.84 cents per kWh, which is below what any of the twelve hosting providers surveyed on this site publishes, and below the US industrial average of 8.71 cents.

Is Bitcoin mining worth it at home?

Not on a residential meter, at these inputs. At the US residential average of 18.44 cents per kWh in May 2026, this model produces a bitcoin for $229,161, which is 3.6 times the market price of $63,050. Every machine hour at that rate destroys value, and no hardware choice repairs it: the best machine in the catalogue on this measure, the SealMiner A4 Pro Air, still needs power below 4.84 cents. Home mining can still make sense as heat, as a hobby, or where the electricity is genuinely free, but it does not make sense as an investment on a metered residential tariff.

How long does a Bitcoin miner take to pay for itself?

At 3.6 cents per kWh, 578 days for a fifty unit Antminer S21 Pro fleet costing $96,900, modelled from 16 August 2026 with difficulty drifting at 0.20 percent per epoch and the subsidy halving on day 607. At 7.5 cents the same fleet does not pay for itself at all inside five years. Payback is not capital divided by this month's profit: this month's profit is the best month the fleet will ever have, because difficulty rises and the block subsidy halves inside any hold longer than 20 months.

What electricity price do you need for Bitcoin mining to be profitable?

Below 3.84 cents per kWh for this configuration, as modelled on 16 August 2026. That is the rate at which the cost to produce one bitcoin, counting power, pool fee and the hardware actually consumed over a 36 month hold, meets the market price of $63,050. The figure moves with the machine: the SealMiner A4 Pro Air tolerates 4.84 cents, the Antminer S21 only 3.26 cents. For reference, Riot discloses 3.6 cents, MARA 4.0 and CleanSpark 5.2 in their 2026 quarterly filings.

Is hosted mining better than mining at home?

On price, almost always. On the model's own venue assumptions, barely at all. Holding the power price at 7.5 cents and changing nothing but the building, hosted produces a bitcoin for $104,654 and home for $105,127, a difference of $474 a coin, because the four extra points of uptime a staffed facility buys very nearly cancel the five extra points of PUE it bills you for. The real argument for hosting is not the building, it is that hosting is how a fifty machine buyer reaches a 5.5 to 8.5 cent rate at all, against 18.44 cents on a residential meter.
