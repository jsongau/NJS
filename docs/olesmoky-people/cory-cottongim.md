# Cory Cottongim
**Co-founder, Ole Smoky Distillery** · founded 2010 · continuing significant shareholder after the 2022 Apax transaction

## Confirmed

- Co-founded Ole Smoky with Joe Baker and Tony Breeden. The distillery launched on 4 July 2010 in Gatlinburg. Source: https://www.distillerytrail.com/blog/americas-no-1-most-visited-distillery-ole-smoky-distillery-has-been-acquired/ and https://alumni.utk.edu/joe-baker-01-and-jessi-baker-03/.
- Named alongside Joe Baker in the Apax announcement as a founder who remains a significant shareholder after the change of control. Source: https://www.apax.com/news-views/apax-funds-to-acquire-controlling-stake-in-ole-smoky-distillery-from-centerview-capita/ (11 February 2022).
- Part of the founding group that developed the original recipes with Joe and Jessi Baker, Tony Breeden and Chuck Edwards. Source: https://alumni.utk.edu/joe-baker-01-and-jessi-baker-03/.
- The founding families have a moonshine history in the Smoky Mountains predating Tennessee statehood, which is the company's stated heritage claim. Source: https://www.distillerytrail.com/blog/americas-no-1-most-visited-distillery-ole-smoky-distillery-has-been-acquired/.

## How they would read a loyalty and CRM work sample

Treat him as a shareholder and a cultural check rather than an evaluator. He is one of two founders the majority owner named as still holding meaningful equity, which means he has a financial interest in exit value and a personal interest in the brand not being flattened into a generic consumer app. Those two interests point the same way here: a first party member base raises the asset value, and it only works if it sounds like Ole Smoky.

The practical implication is narrow but real. If the loyalty programme's language, badges and rituals sound like they came from a template, the people who built the brand will notice before anyone else does. The specific decisions that would read correctly to a founder are the ones that keep the local vocabulary honest rather than decorative: quest names that state the action plainly, badge names that carry the romance, and nothing borrowed from a competitor's trademark. Two badge names had to be cut for exactly that reason, because Devil's Cut belongs to Jim Beam and Whistle Pig to WhistlePig Rye. Getting that wrong in front of the people who named the original products is avoidable and expensive.

## What makes them say no

- Brand language that could belong to any company.
- Anything that dilutes the Gatlinburg and East Tennessee origin story.
- A programme that treats the distillery as a retail unit rather than the thing the company was built around.
- Trademark carelessness in naming.
